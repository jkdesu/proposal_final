Volatility of Everyday Life

Measuring Emotional Response to Cross-City Price Differences

Ji-In Kang • Columbia GSAPP • December 2025
01

Research Question & Hypothesis

"How can real-time visual tools be designed to measure and communicate price differences in daily consumer goods between cities, and how do individuals emotionally respond to these differences when presented dynamically?"

Through a comparison between Tokyo and New York City, this project investigates how people react to the price changes of the same item.

Hypothesis

When real-time visual tools reveal price differences between Tokyo and New York City, individuals will display stronger emotional reactions to goods with higher perceived price elasticity—everyday essentials whose prices are expected to remain stable. This suggests that emotional response patterns can function as a behavioral measure of elasticity, revealing how sensitive people feel to unexpected price changes.

02

Project Description

My project investigates how people react to price differences between Tokyo and New York by combining real-time visualization with facial-expression analytics.

After spending most of my adulthood in both cities, I became curious about how living expenses shape people's perceptions—and how individuals emotionally respond when confronted with drastically different prices for the same everyday goods.

To explore this, the project visualizes price volatility in two ways:

Economic Volatility

The changing cost of identical consumer items across cities, shown through interactive simulations and cumulative cost-of-living calculators.

Human Volatility

The rate of change in a user's facial expression when they encounter unexpected or extreme price differences, measured through real-time face-tracking.

By integrating cost-of-living simulation with emotional data, the project examines not only how much prices vary between Tokyo and NYC, but also how people physically react to these economic contrasts. Ultimately, it aims to understand how individuals process affordability differences—not just rationally, but emotionally.

03

Computational Methods

My project uses browser-based computer vision, dynamic data rendering, and interactive simulation to analyze how users emotionally respond to cross-city price differences.

Real-time facial-expression tracking quantifies volatility as a rate of change in users' reactions, turning emotional responses into measurable data. Simultaneously, a scroll-driven price engine and cumulative budget calculator visualize how daily expenses accumulate in Tokyo and New York.

These computational methods enable an experiential analysis: they reveal not only the numerical gaps between cities but also how people physically and intuitively process those gaps. The workflow transforms static economic data into an interactive, human-centered interpretation of affordability.

04

Design Methods

My design methods are primarily experimental, analytic, and narrative, using interaction to expose how economic information shapes emotional experience.

By pairing real-time price visualization with facial-expression tracking, the project creates a participatory environment where users become active subjects in the analysis rather than passive observers.

This approach makes it possible to tell a human-centered economic story, revealing how people intuitively react to affordability gaps between Tokyo and New York.

Experimental

Creating novel interactions between economic data and emotional response

Analytic

Quantifying emotional reactions as measurable data points

Narrative

Telling a human-centered story through interactive visualization

Critical

Challenging the assumption that economic data is objective or detached

The design also has a critical dimension, challenging the assumption that economic data is objective or detached by showing that perceptions of cost are deeply embodied and affective. Through simulation and interaction, the work highlights how data, emotion, and lived experience intersect in everyday urban life.

05

Precedents

The Economist — Big Mac Index & PPP Calculator

The Economist's Big Mac Index is a key precedent for my project because it uses a single, everyday commodity to reveal global price disparities through a playful yet rigorous economic lens.

Its PPP calculator demonstrates how a familiar good can serve as a microeconomic indicator of affordability across cities. My project extends this idea by shifting from a static comparison tool to an interactive, real-time emotional and computational experience.

Rather than only displaying price differences, my project measures how people feel and react to them, critiquing the assumption that cost-of-living data is purely rational and proposing an alternative: that affordability is both economic and emotional.

Future Development: Spatial Experience

For my final project, I aim to create a spatial experience where people can scroll through items spatially where all the images are portrayed on the wall. I want to have separate rooms for Tokyo and NYC so that people actually feel like they are experiencing being in two different cities.

I want this project to expand more spatially because I think having a physical experience will have more impact on the rate of change in facial expression rather than looking at items on the screen.

07

Audience

Primary Audience

My primary audience consists of individuals who have lived in or traveled between major global cities because they carry personal context for interpreting price differences. For this group, the project resonates as both economic analysis and lived experience.

Accessibility Considerations

The project may be less accessible to audiences unfamiliar with cost-of-living issues, median income disparities, or facial-tracking interfaces.

Who might be excluded?

  • Those unfamiliar with international cost-of-living comparisons
  • Users uncomfortable with webcam/facial tracking
  • People without experience in either Tokyo or NYC

Alternative Designs

  • Educational onboarding explaining wage/price context
  • Non-camera-based emotional input options
  • City selection beyond Tokyo/NYC for broader relevance

Data

Scraped Price Data

Official price information from major commercial businesses common to both cities, including coffee chains, convenience stores, fast food, and grocery staples. All businesses selected exist at a multinational level.

Wage and Income Data

Average wages in each city reported by NYC report and Tokyo. Includes disposable income estimations (wage → taxes → essential payments).

Essential Expenses

Median rent, utilities, and transportation costs in both cities to calculate baseline living expenses.

Big Mac Index as Precedent

The Big Mac Index is a valuable precedent as it compares the price level of goods between countries. It indicates whether currencies are overvalued or undervalued, reflecting market prices (Transfer Pricing). This project extends this methodology to a broader range of everyday consumer goods.

Annotated Bibliography

01

Tokyo Metropolitan Government. "Statistical Yearbook of Tokyo."

Offers official statistics on wages, living costs, transportation and household expenditures in Tokyo. This provides the foundational economic data for the Tokyo side of the comparison, ensuring accuracy and reliability in price representations.

02

The Economist. "The Big Mac Index."

This index compares purchasing power across countries using the price of a single, standardized commodity. It provides the conceptual foundation for treating everyday consumer goods as microeconomic indicators, which my project extends through interactive visualization and emotional analysis.

03

Edward R. Tufte. The Visual Display of Quantitative Information.

Tufte's principles of clarity, density, and visual reasoning provide a theoretical foundation for design choices. His work helps justify my decision to use dynamic, interactive graphics to communicate volatility and price differences effectively.

04

Daniel Kahneman. Thinking, Fast and Slow.

Kahneman's work on cognitive biases and intuitive reactions informs my project's focus on emotional response to economic information. It supports the idea that people process price differences affectively, not just rationally.

05

Rosalind W. Picard. Affective Computing.

Picard introduces the theoretical basis for measuring emotion through computational systems. This text frames the use of facial-expression tracking as part of the methodology for analyzing "emotional volatility" as a rate of change in user reactions.

06

Paul Ekman. Emotions Revealed: Recognizing Faces and Feelings.

Ekman's foundational research on facial expressions and universal emotions provides the scientific basis for facial-expression analysis used in this project. His work on micro-expressions informs how subtle emotional changes can be detected and measured.

07

Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein. Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness.

This work on behavioral economics and choice architecture informs how price information can be designed to influence perception. The project applies these principles to understand how presentation of cost data affects emotional response.

08

Dan Ariely. Predictably Irrational: The Hidden Forces That Shape Our Decisions.

Ariely's research on irrational economic behavior provides context for understanding why people react emotionally to price differences. His work on the psychology of pricing directly relates to the project's investigation of emotional volatility in response to cost comparisons.

Data Sources

Tokyo Metropolitan Government Statistics

toukei.metro.tokyo.lg.jp

Official government statistics providing accurate wage, rent, and cost-of-living data for Tokyo. Published annually with transparent methodology and data collection practices by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government.

NYC Open Data / Bureau of Labor Statistics

data.cityofnewyork.us / bls.gov

Official U.S. government data on wages, consumer prices, and living costs in the New York metropolitan area. Data is collected through systematic surveys and published with clear provenance.

The Economist Big Mac Index

economist.com/big-mac-index

A semi-annual publication comparing Big Mac prices globally. While playful, it represents a rigorous attempt to compare purchasing power parity across countries using a standardized commodity.

Corporate Price Data (Scraped)

Various multinational retailers

Prices collected from official websites and menus of multinational businesses operating in both Tokyo and NYC (coffee chains, convenience stores, fast food). Data reflects publicly displayed prices as of 2025.

Numbeo Cost of Living Database

numbeo.com

Crowdsourced cost-of-living data used for cross-validation. While user-contributed, it provides broad coverage and is useful for identifying trends and verifying official statistics.

Notes on Data Politics

All data sources reflect specific institutional perspectives and collection methodologies. Government statistics may not capture informal economies or recent price fluctuations. Crowdsourced data may over-represent certain demographics. The project acknowledges these limitations and presents comparisons as illustrative rather than definitive measures of lived experience.
